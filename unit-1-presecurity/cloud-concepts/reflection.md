Cloud in your own words

You know how you can rent a storage unit instead of buying a bigger house? Cloud computing is kind of like that, but for computers. You rent storage, computing power, software, and other resources from a company that owns the physical machines, and you usually only pay for what you use. You do not need to buy expensive servers or maintain the hardware yourself; you just connect to the service and use what you need. You can also increase or decrease the resources when your needs change.

Traditional → Cloud → Containers

Every step in this development is about finding a better way to use computers and make IT easier to manage. In traditional IT, one physical server might run one main application, which could leave resources unused. Virtualization improved this by allowing one physical machine to run several virtual machines at the same time. Cloud computing took this further by letting organisations rent computing resources over the internet instead of buying and maintaining all the hardware themselves. Containers made applications more lightweight and portable because they can share the host operating system instead of needing a complete operating system for every application. Each step improved efficiency and flexibility but also introduced new things for IT workers to learn.

Deployment vs Service models

A deployment model is about where the infrastructure is located and who has access to it, such as public cloud, private cloud, on-premises, hybrid, or multiple cloud environments. A service model describes how much of the technology the customer manages compared with the provider. IaaS gives the customer more control, PaaS manages more of the underlying environment, and SaaS is ready-to-use software. A simple example of public cloud plus SaaS is Gmail because Google manages the infrastructure while I simply log in and use the email application.

The Shared Responsibility Model

The Shared Responsibility Model means that security is divided between the cloud provider and the customer. The provider is responsible for things like physical servers, data centres, and the infrastructure they operate, while the customer is still responsible for their data, accounts, passwords, MFA, permissions, and configuration. This applies to IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS, although the exact responsibilities change depending on the service. A real example is the Capital One breach in 2019, where a misconfigured web application firewall allowed an attacker to access customer information stored in AWS. AWS was not simply hacked; the incident showed that customer configuration can create a serious security problem even when the provider's infrastructure is working normally.

Why organisations still hesitate

One reason organisations may hesitate to move everything to the cloud is regulations such as GDPR. Companies handling personal or sensitive information may have requirements about how and where that data is stored and processed. Another reason is money and existing investments. A company may already have spent a lot on its own servers and networking equipment, so moving everything to the cloud may not immediately make financial sense. Some organisations may also worry about becoming too dependent on one cloud provider.

Cloud in an entry-level tech role

If I worked in helpdesk, a normal ticket could be someone saying they cannot log in to Microsoft 365. The problem might not be their computer at all; it could be their account, MFA, password, permissions, or another identity issue. If I worked as a junior sysadmin, I might receive an alert about a strange sign-in attempt on an employee's cloud account and need to check whether the account has been compromised. These situations show that cloud knowledge is useful even in entry-level IT jobs because many everyday support and security problems are connected to cloud services.
